
Reconstructing the Hush Product Thesis
After thirteen specifications, the thesis can be stated more precisely than the Founder Package did — and the precision matters, because the entire risk profile turns on five concepts that the spec stack uses almost interchangeably but which are not the same thing.
The five concepts, properly distinguished
These exist on a ladder. Each rung requires the one below it and adds something the lower rung lacks.
Recommendation — an output. The system computes "do this exercise, at this weight, for these reps" (ES-006). A recommendation is a claim about what is best, emitted regardless of whether anyone follows it. It costs the athlete nothing and commits them to nothing. The system produces recommendations from observation one; their existence is not contingent on trust, authority, or anything else. A recommendation is information.
Decision — an output that is acted upon as binding. A recommendation becomes a decision when the athlete executes it without independently evaluating it — when they do it because Hush said so, not because they checked and agreed. The Founder Package's North Star ("% executed without override") is really measuring the recommendation→decision conversion rate. A decision is a recommendation the athlete has stopped scrutinizing. This is the rung where Hush's stated ambition lives: "Hush's job is to decide."
Trust — the justified basis for the conversion. Trust is what should turn a recommendation into a decision: the athlete's warranted belief, built from accumulated accuracy, that following is better than deliberating. ES-012 defined it precisely as accuracy × costly-deference × retained-discernment. Crucially, trust is the earned version of the recommendation→decision conversion. The unearned version is compliance (following without justification) — and the two are behaviorally identical, which is the measurement crisis at the heart of the Red Team Review.
Authority — the standing to have recommendations treated as decisions by default. Authority is trust that has become structural — the athlete no longer re-grants it per recommendation; it is the default posture. Where trust is evaluated ("was this one right?"), authority is assumed ("Hush decides; I train"). The Founder Package's deepest ambition — "Hush earns the right to make decisions on the athlete's behalf" — is a claim about authority, not trust. Authority is what lets the system decide on the next, unseen recommendation, before any evidence about that specific recommendation exists.
Override — the athlete reclaiming the decision. An override is the athlete declining to convert a recommendation into a decision — reasserting that this output will be scrutinized and possibly rejected (ES-010). An override is the revocation event. It is simultaneously the system's richest learning signal (ES-010: override-as-evidence) and the visible withdrawal of authority. This dual nature — overrides are both good (data) and bad (authority erosion) — is the contradiction ES-006 flagged and ES-012 partially resolved.
The thesis, stated precisely
Hush produces recommendations, seeks to convert them into decisions, justifies that conversion through trust, and aims to consolidate that trust into standing authority — such that the athlete delegates not just individual choices but the category of training decisions, permanently.
The product is not "good workout recommendations." The product is the transfer of authority — the moment the athlete stops evaluating and starts delegating, and that delegation persists into recommendations not yet made. Everything in the model exists to manufacture and protect that transfer: conservative seeds protect it from early failure, the audit chain makes it explainable, fatigue modeling prevents false load-drops from breaking it, ES-012 measures whether it's earned rather than merely extracted.
And here is the precise location of the existential risk, now that the concepts are separated: the model is built to achieve authority but can only measure trust, and can only measure trust through behavior that is indistinguishable from mere compliance. Authority (deciding the next recommendation correctly) is a claim about the future that no amount of past data validates. The Red Team's A1–A4 are all, at root, the same problem: you cannot validate authority, only observe compliance, and compliance is what both authority and resignation produce.

What changes if the goal is "Become the default recommendation"
Reframing the ambition from "take over decision making" (authority) to "become the athlete's default recommendation" (a strong, sticky recommendation the athlete usually follows but always owns) moves Hush down the ladder — from the authority rung to the recommendation-with-stickiness rung. The athlete still decides; Hush is just the trusted first suggestion. This is a smaller claim, and the consequences for the assumptions register are dramatic and mostly favorable.
Assumptions that DISAPPEAR entirely
A4 — Willingness to delegate. Gone. "Default recommendation" requires no delegation of authority — it requires only that the athlete find Hush's suggestion good enough to usually accept, while retaining ownership. People already accept default recommendations constantly (search results, map routes, streaming queues) without delegating authority. The existential question "will athletes hand over decision-making?" simply doesn't arise. This is the single biggest change: one of the four existential assumptions evaporates.
A3 — Trust measurability through costly deference. Largely gone. The whole apparatus of distinguishing earned trust from passive compliance existed because authority requires earned justification — a decider must deserve it. A default recommendation does not need to be obeyed on hard, counterintuitive calls; it needs to be useful enough to remain the default. The metric becomes "does the athlete keep coming back to Hush's suggestion" — ordinary retention and preference, which are measurable without manufacturing costly-case events. DiscernmentRetained, the unobservable-in-principle factor, becomes irrelevant: you want the athlete discerning, because discernment + continued use is exactly what "good default" means.
Assumptions that become STRONGER (better supported / lower risk)
A2 — Loop convergence. Strengthened in consequence, not in truth. The loop still might drift, but the stakes of drift collapse: a drifting recommendation the athlete evaluates is self-correcting (they override the bad ones), whereas a drifting decision the athlete has stopped scrutinizing compounds silently. The override channel becomes a safety valve that's actually used. Convergence matters less because the human stays in the loop. Risk: Existential → Medium.
A1 — Capability recoverability. Strengthened in consequence. If capability estimates are wrong, a recommendation that's slightly off gets corrected by an attentive athlete; a decision that's wrong harms someone who stopped checking. The unobservability of true capability remains, but its blast radius shrinks because the athlete is a continuous error-correction mechanism rather than a passive recipient. Still un-falsifiable in principle, but no longer existential. Risk: Existential → High.
A9 — Override interpretability. Strengthened, and its meaning inverts. Under "authority," overrides are authority-erosion to be minimized. Under "default recommendation," overrides are the athlete using the product correctly — they are expected, healthy, and the primary learning signal with no downside. Off-catalog overrides still create attribution gaps, but the gap is now "we learned less from this one," not "the athlete is rejecting our authority." Lower stakes.
A7 — Seed safety. Marginally strengthened. First-session failure is still bad, but its consequence shifts from "broke the authority transfer permanently" to "gave a bad first suggestion the athlete corrected." A default recommendation survives a wrong first guess; an authority claim often doesn't. Still High (injury risk is real regardless), but the trust-destruction component softens.
Assumptions that become WEAKER (more exposed / higher relative risk)
A8 — De-biased error as model quality signal. Becomes more central, not less — and that's a weakening of your position, because it's the assumption you can least validate at MVP scale. When Hush was claiming authority, it had a grand thesis (delegation) to validate; if that succeeded, recommendation quality was somewhat assumed. As "just the default recommendation," Hush has only recommendation quality to stand on. The product lives or dies on whether the suggestions are actually good — and A8 (can we even measure that honestly?) plus the underpowered shadow baseline (A8/§6.4) become the whole ballgame. The bar rises: a default recommendation must be demonstrably better than the alternative the athlete would have chosen, or there's no reason to keep it as default. Risk: High → High, but now load-bearing in a way it wasn't.
A14 — Exploration / preference lock-in. Becomes more important. A "default recommendation" product is judged on continued usefulness over months; preference lock-in that narrows the experience degrades exactly the long-run stickiness the new goal depends on. Under "authority," lock-in was a quality issue; under "default," it's a retention issue, which is the core metric. Relative risk up.
A13 — Volume bands / A10 κ / A11 σ²_ref. Slightly more exposed, for the same reason as A8: with the grand delegation thesis removed, the unglamorous question "are the actual numbers good?" carries more weight. These were tolerable approximations under a thesis that was mostly about behavior; under a thesis that's entirely about recommendation quality, approximation error is more directly felt.
Assumptions essentially UNCHANGED
A5, A6 (effort/fatigue identifiability, τ learning), A12, A16–A18 — these are internal model-quality assumptions. They affect how good the recommendation is, and they're equally relevant whether the recommendation is a "default" or a "decision." Reframing the goal doesn't touch them. (A6's recommendation to ship fixed-τ stands regardless.)

The reframing doesn't make Hush a smaller product — it makes it a validatable one. The "authority" framing put the entire company behind four assumptions the MVP structurally cannot test (Red Team's central finding: risk and testability are inversely correlated). The "default recommendation" framing collapses three of those four existential assumptions and converts the fourth (A1) from "unfalsifiable in principle" to "lower-stakes because the human corrects errors." What remains is an honest, testable claim: are the recommendations good enough that athletes keep using them by default? — which retention and the shadow baseline can actually answer at N=10–100.
The strategic observation
There is a real tension here worth naming, not hiding. The "authority" thesis is the more valuable one if it's true — owning the decision category is a deeper moat than being a good suggestion. The "default recommendation" thesis is the more validatable one and fails gracefully. These are not the same bet.
But notice what the spec stack actually built: a system whose every safety mechanism (conservative defaults, KEEP_LOAD bias, override-as-welcome-evidence, the human always able to reassert) is already the architecture of a default-recommendation product, not an authority product. An authority product would minimize overrides as erosion; Hush treats them as data. An authority product would push hard calls to prove its standing; Hush avoids them for safety. The model you froze is, in its bones, a default-recommendation engine wearing an authority thesis. The Red Team Review's deepest contradiction (A3/§1.4: the system is built to avoid the costly events its authority metric requires) dissolves the moment you accept that the system was never really built for authority — it was built to be the trusted default, and the authority language was aspiration laid over a more modest, more honest machine.
My recommendation, as Chief Scientist: adopt "become the default recommendation" as the validated MVP thesis, and treat "take over decision-making" as the long-horizon hypothesis you earn the right to test only after the default-recommendation version has proven the recommendations are genuinely good. You cannot validate authority on an athlete who doesn't yet have reason to grant it. Earn the default first. Authority, if it comes, is what a years-long track record of being the right default eventually becomes — which is exactly what Founder Principle 8 ("the first month is for learning, the following years for optimizing") was always implying, even if the North Star metric forgot it.

Thesis v2 already targets the lower, validatable rung ('a good, sticky default') and defers AUTHORITY to a later phase. This addendum records how the v1 build realizes that choice.
The MVP goal, as built, is: understand training progress, DETECT stagnation, and help build better future programs (Product Specification §1) — NOT to take over the working-load decision. Hush recommends; the athlete decides. The recommended load is advisory; the athlete owns load.
The five-rung ladder (Recommendation → Decision → Trust → Authority → Override) is unchanged. v1 validates the lower rungs only: a trustworthy, honest default that learns from overrides. Authority remains a Phase-3 claim, untestable at MVP N.
